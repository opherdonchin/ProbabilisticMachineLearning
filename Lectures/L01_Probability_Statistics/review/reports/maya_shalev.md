### 1. Maya's experience of the lecture

I experienced all 67 rendered pages in sequence. `slide_deck.typ` was rendered at run time from the unchanged source at the stated commit; the supplied rendering had no compilation warnings. The reactions below are to the rendered lecture, without access to its source or lecturer notes.

**A familiar place to start, slides 1–9.** I arrive expecting to find out what machine learning adds to the Bayesian statistics I already know. The coin model makes me comfortable. I do not need to care about coins to recognize an observation model and an unknown probability. I briefly substitute success on a balance task for heads, then immediately remember that repeated trials in a person could change with fatigue. That is my qualification to the analogy, rather than a reason to reject the coin example. The distinction between observed and unknown quantities on slide 3 is the important part.

The graph on slide 5 is appealing: I could use this to make assumptions visible in a lab meeting. I register that arrows mean conditional dependence, although my first instinct is still to read a downward arrow as a physical production process. I do not write down the remark about Kruschke diagrams. The conjugate update is revision, and my attention loosens a little by the summary. The plotted prior and posterior on slide 8 are easier to take in than the smaller distributions on slide 4. I read the claim about reduced uncertainty as describing this update; I am not trying to establish a general theorem about every dataset.

**Whose question are we answering, slides 10–22?** Slide 11 sounds like my research: infer parameters and ask what they mean. I feel recognized by “for many scientific questions, that posterior is the answer.” The input/output contract on slide 12 initially sounds more instrumental than scientific. But I can see that a system estimating a quantity in real time can be useful even if the implementation does not explain the body. I am willing to hear the distinction; I am not yet persuaded that statistics and machine learning divide this neatly.

I look at the retina in the historical perceptron drawing on slide 13, because it catches my neuroscience interest, but the lecturer's point seems to be the shared classification interface, not a model of vision. I let that interest go. On slide 14 I understand the sentence about task, experience, and performance better than the several functions underneath it. I can follow each arrow if I stop, but I would not reconstruct the whole chain from memory. I write the three ordinary words and leave the subscripts alone.

Iris is a good example for me. The measurements on slide 16 make clear that the inputs are four measured lengths, not flower photographs. The pair plot on slide 17 lets me judge what a classifier has to work with, including the overlapping classes. “Always inspect data before modeling” reassures me that the lecturer has not forgotten the observations. Fifty flowers per class makes me wonder what population the class probabilities will represent, but I do not need that settled to understand the exercise.

The loss equations on slides 18–19 now have a job. Zero-one loss means deciding that all wrong labels incur the same cost. I mentally contrast that with missing a dangerous event versus raising a false alarm in a movement experiment. I would write “performance measure is a choice,” although those exact words are mine. I mostly ignore the small empirical-distribution formula on slide 18: the average loss above it is enough for my present understanding. Bayes' rule on slide 20 is the first convincing bridge back to the opening. The distribution of measurements within a class can be turned into a probability of a class given measurements. This is something I know how to reason about. I still wonder whether choosing the largest probability is automatically the right action when errors differ in consequence. The phrase “a possible classifier” leaves room for that question.

At slide 21, training misclassification rate as performance bothers me. A model that labels the people it has already seen is not necessarily useful on a new participant. I carry that question forward rather than conclude that the course will ignore it.

**The taxonomy becomes useful unevenly, slides 23–35.** Separating what the learner observes from what we want it to do is a real distinction. We may have a large amount of movement data and very little reliable annotation; those are different constraints from the eventual research question. Self-supervised learning is new vocabulary for me. I tentatively imagine hiding part of a recording and predicting it from the rest, but I do not leave this sequence knowing how to build such a learner.

Slide 25 is where I stop reading every symbol. The prose descriptions of prediction, generation, discovery, and action survive; the detailed mappings do not. “Discovery” attracts me because I want structure that might mean something physiologically. I momentarily hear more in that word than “find a representation of the data” actually promises. I know PCA does not automatically reveal a biological control variable, but I am still hoping that this category will lead there.

The table on slide 26 is easier to use. I could plausibly copy it as an examination aid. I am not entirely sure whether the dashes mean impossible combinations or simply combinations that are not being illustrated. The self-supervised row at least prevents me from treating training regime and task as the same taxonomy.

The familiar-dataset tour is less compelling than Iris, but not because digits and reviews are outside biology. MNIST on slide 30 makes a useful point: the same observations could support recognition, a representation, or generation. A folder of trials is likewise not yet a research question. CIFAR's train/test distinction on slide 31 answers my earlier concern at the broad level. It does not tell me what constitutes a new case when many trials come from one subject, and I retain that as a future practical question. By the review-text slide I am skimming the repeated prediction/discovery boxes. I understand what is being contrasted without understanding much more about either discovery or generation.

Slides 33–35 try to collect this into probabilistic queries. I like “variables available” and “query we want answered.” The three probability operations are familiar individually, but their status as a summary feels slightly ahead of my experience: I have not yet seen all those ML task boxes become actual computations. I assume the Gaussian section is where that promise will be made concrete.

**The mathematics acquires a use, slides 36–54.** The density on slide 37 is recognizable, and the picture on slide 38 does more for me than another definition could. Marginalizing is allowing the unobserved coordinate to vary; conditioning uses its observed value. I imagine one measured kinematic quantity helping infer another. I can hold the slice and the projection apart visually.

Covariance and correlation are familiar, but slides 39–43 sharpen the geometry. Units matter in my lab, so the correlation/scale separation and the transformation rule are not decorative algebra to me. I follow the covariance proof on slide 41 and write down `A Sigma A^T`. The Mahalanobis distance means that a deviation along the cloud's usual variation is different from one across it. I can imagine using that to describe an unusual movement pattern. I also know that unusual is not the same as pathological. The three contour shapes make the covariance restrictions tangible.

The block notation on slide 46 needs a conscious adjustment: each of the two “coordinates” can itself be a group of measurements. The marginal rule is manageable. I recognize the conditional mean on slide 48 as a regression-like correction, but tracking the residual argument takes work. I follow its main logic rather than retain every line. The explicit jointly Gaussian qualification matters: it tells me why zero residual covariance is being allowed to imply independence here, after slide 39 warned against that inference in general.

Slide 49 makes me pause for a different reason. Under this model, the conditional covariance does not depend on the actual observed value. That is a substantial assumption when measurement reliability can worsen at extremes of movement. I can understand the formula and still doubt whether it fits a particular experiment. The scalar formula and the calculation to mean 1.2 and variance 0.36 on slides 50–51 give me back a firm grip after the matrix proof. I would reproduce that calculation in my notes.

“Schur complement” on slide 52 sounds like something examinable. “Residual covariance” is what gives it meaning. I understand what remains after accounting for the observed variables; I do not yet have much personal use for the block-elimination name. By slide 53 I am tired of following alternative algebraic descriptions of the same Gaussian. I accept the factorization but would need to revisit the quadratic identity to derive it. That is a local drop in attention, not losing the whole argument.

**The measurement model is the payoff, slides 55–67.** “State and measurement” on slide 56 immediately makes me sit forward. This is the distinction I keep asking for: what exists or is modeled underneath, what the instrument records, and how they are related. The graph is now more useful than it was with the coin. I start imagining a physical state behind a noisy measurement. I also notice that all the parameters are given. We are doing inference within a model here, not yet learning the measurement law from our experimental data.

Slide 57 puts the three operations into questions I might actually ask. The later joint covariance blocks are a lot of symbols, but I can see why the earlier transformation and conditioning rules were needed. On slide 60 the gain turns an observed-minus-predicted measurement into a correction of state. My control-systems background makes that satisfying. I briefly think of sensory correction during movement, then remind myself that a mathematical update is not evidence that the nervous system implements this model.

The scalar derivation and three uncertainty cases on slides 61–62 are particularly memorable. I could explain why a noisier observation should move the estimate less. “Condition, then summarize” on slide 63 is also useful: an estimated state and uncertainty about that state are not interchangeable outputs.

Sensor fusion on slide 64 is the example I would discuss afterward. The third picture surprises me in a good way: taking the more reliable direction from each measurement can put the estimate away from the straight line between them. This is why a covariance matrix is more than a confidence score for an entire sensor. But I also stop trusting the drawing literally. We have just spent several slides on a prior, and now I cannot see its role. Would equal sensor uncertainty still put the posterior between them if the prior were strongly elsewhere? And do the two sensors share error? I cannot tell from the two conditional distributions alone.

I would not simply put EMG and a force reading into the two-sensor picture: they do not measure the same quantity. The earlier `Wz+b` law gives me a way to think about that distinction, even though choosing and validating that law is still ahead of us. Slide 65 adds another useful question: what measurements should this model produce before we observe them? I connect that to checking whether a proposed model could plausibly generate our data. The slide presents the predictive distribution, not a completed model-checking procedure, but the connection is available to me.

At the end I feel I have learned a coherent inference language, with a stretch of ML vocabulary whose usefulness is less evenly established. The last visual anchors bring the two halves together enough that I do not experience them as unrelated lectures. My strongest memory is how uncertainty changes the use of a measurement, not the list of benchmark datasets.

### 2. Lab-meeting version

“The useful part was separating our measurements from the state we want to infer. If we can defend a measurement model, we can combine a prior with observations and retain uncertainty instead of just producing a cleaned signal. For a linear Gaussian model, the correction is the discrepancy between the actual and predicted measurement, multiplied by a gain determined by covariance. A noisy measurement should have less influence. With several dimensions, a sensor can be useful in one direction and poor in another; there isn't necessarily one reliability weight for the whole sensor.

“I also took away that we need to specify what we want a learned system to do before saying we will apply ML to our recordings. Predicting a label, finding a representation, and producing plausible recordings are different tasks even with the same dataset. The loss function says what kinds of error we count. That seems relevant before we decide what to label or what performance to report.

“I am not claiming that the latent state in one of these models is automatically a physiological mechanism. And the worked Gaussian model assumes the measurement law and its uncertainty are known. For our data, estimating and checking those assumptions may be the difficult part. I'd want to know how to deal with correlated sensor errors, different subjects, and noise that changes with the state. But I can now state more precisely what distribution we want after seeing a measurement, and what measurements the model predicts before seeing one.”

### 3. What Maya retains

The vocabulary I would actually use is **measurement model, latent state, conditional distribution, residual covariance, gain, and direction-dependent uncertainty**. Some of those terms were already familiar; the lecture has connected them. “A dataset is not a task” also stays with me. Task, available experience, and performance is a useful way to make a vague research plan answerable.

I retain the Gaussian slice versus marginal picture, the numerical conditioning example, and especially the anisotropic sensor-fusion picture. Iris remains a successful example because I saw the measurements and their joint variation. The coin served as a comfortable starting point. I will probably forget the dataset ages, the historical dates, and the precise distinctions among the repeated task-function symbols. Schur complement will be a name I can recognize before it is a tool I can use fluently. Self-supervised learning and generation remain promising categories rather than methods I understand operationally.

My notes would contain the affine covariance rule, the conditional mean and covariance, the scalar gain, and a reminder that Gaussian conditional covariance does not change with the measured value. I would expect those formulas and the taxonomy to be examinable. I would need to revisit the residual proofs and the quadratic decomposition before an exam; familiarity with the result is not the same as being able to derive it.

Questions I want answered later are:

- How do we learn the measurement law and distinguish state variation from measurement error?
- What changes when subjects differ, noise depends on state, or sensors have correlated errors?
- How should we evaluate on new people rather than merely new trials?
- When is a learned representation evidence about biology, and when is it only a useful compression?
- How do task costs affect the decision after we have a predictive distribution?

### 4. Analyst's assessment

**Successful connection and broadening.** Maya's strongest engagement comes from the full path through Gaussian geometry, conditional inference, and measurement models, not simply the appearance of sensors at the end. The affine rule and conditional covariance acquire retrospective purpose on slides 56–65. The lecture also broadens her view of ML beyond classification: one dataset can support several tasks, and useful inference can be distinguished from a point estimate. Iris succeeds on its own terms. Her response does not support a diagnosis that the lecture needs biomedical examples to motivate her.

**A genuine unevenness in motivation.** In slides 24–35, the symbolic task taxonomies outrun the concrete meaning she can attach to several categories. She understands the distinctions in prose but mostly bypasses the mappings on slides 25 and 33. Repeating the boxes across datasets consolidates the names without giving her a comparable experience of discovery or generation. Their status remains less secure than classification or Gaussian inference. This is a connection gap for a mathematically competent student with limited ML background, rather than aversion to mathematics. The function notation on slide 14 has a similar cost without equal benefit for her.

**Productive difficulty.** The conditioning proofs require effort but reward it. Slides 50–51 restore concrete understanding after the block algebra, and slide 52 gives a meaningful interpretation to the Schur complement. Maya's inability to reproduce slide 53 immediately is not itself a lecture failure. Her attention falls there because she no longer sees an additional scientific question being answered, but the mathematical connection remains legitimate. No claim about actual lecture duration follows from this rendered-only experience.

**Maya's priorities and overinterpretations.** Her desire for interpretable mechanisms makes slide 11 unusually congenial and initially makes the computational-contract perspective seem less scientific. That is partly her bias: prediction can have scientific or practical value without recovering a mechanism. She also briefly overreads “discovery,” and later sees a possible account of biological feedback in the gain formula. Neither inference is established by the lecture. The rendered text on slide 25 says representation, and slide 56 explicitly treats the model parameters as given. Her eventual distinction between a useful latent state and an established physiological entity is an important self-correction, not an explanation supplied in full by the slides.

**Skepticism that exposes an unstated assumption.** Slide 64 is the sharpest case. Its displayed model includes a prior, but the equal-uncertainty claim and drawing do not state the prior conditions needed for the illustrated posterior location. Equal sensor uncertainty alone does not establish that location for an arbitrary informative prior. Also, specifying each sensor's distribution conditional on the state does not by itself specify their conditional dependence. Maya's concern about shared sensor error therefore identifies missing assumptions needed to interpret the fusion calculation, not just a demand for greater realism. The visually powerful result is also the result she is most likely to reuse.

Her concerns about class proportions on slides 17–20, state-dependent uncertainty on slide 49, and evaluation across people are more appropriately distinguished. The first identifies an unexamined link between sampling and deployment probabilities; the second recognizes a stated Gaussian-model restriction; the third asks for a practical extension beyond the train/test signal on slide 31. It would be unfair to say the lecture ignores evaluation, noise assumptions, or the difference between observations and hidden quantities. It introduces all three. What remains unresolved is how to establish that those assumptions and evaluation choices are defensible for the particular scientific problem.
