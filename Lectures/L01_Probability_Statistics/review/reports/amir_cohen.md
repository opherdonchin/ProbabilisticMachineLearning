## 1. Amir during the lecture

I experienced the 67 rendered pages sequentially. For this review, `slide_deck.typ` was rendered at run time from unchanged source at the stated commit; the supplied compilation had no warnings. I reviewed the rendered pages, not the source.

At the beginning I think this is going to be the probability course coming back to haunt me, but the coin model on slide 3 is a reassuring place to start. I can translate it: an array of zeros and ones, a scalar parameter, two prior settings. On slide 7 I remember the useful part of conjugacy immediately: count the heads and tails and add them to the two parameters. I would write that update down. I would not reconstruct the Beta normalization constant. The graph on slide 5 is readable, although my first instinct is to treat the arrows as a program's data flow. The promise that graphs will help factor complicated problems interests me more than the diagram convention itself.

The prior, likelihood, and posterior curves on slide 8 make the recap feel complete. I register that the likelihood has been scaled, but I do not stop to think through what that means for comparing heights. My retained picture is that data pull the distribution toward the observed fraction and make it narrower. I probably leave this section with a slightly stronger belief than the slide warrants: more observations mean less uncertainty. The filenames below the early plots look like breadcrumbs to runnable examples. The small plot on slide 4 is much less useful to me than the large one on slide 8; I read its general shape, not its legend.

Slides 11–13 initially feel like a distinction between two departments. Statistics wants parameters; computer science wants a function. Fine, but my classifier also had parameters, and statisticians predict things. The input/output contract on slide 12 is very natural to me. The old Fisher and perceptron pictures become interesting because they share that contract, not because I particularly want to study their history. I take the point to be that the problem survives a change of implementation.

Slide 14 almost makes that simple idea harder. I translate training, task, and evaluation to `fit`, `predict`, and `score`, then spend a moment wondering what the extra mapping from experience and performance to the training function means. Is selecting a training procedure itself what is being learned? I do not resolve all the subscripts. Slide 15 rescues me: labeled pairs go in, a predictor comes out, then a new input goes through the predictor. That is concrete enough. I expect these three ingredients to be examinable because they keep returning.

Iris on slides 16–17 is familiar, but I like seeing the actual table and the four-dimensional input. The flower photos alone would have made me think of an image classifier; the table settles what enters this program. The pair plot and the instruction to inspect data before modeling feel earned. I have seen datasets with an impressive model sitting on top of an embarrassing preprocessing error.

At slide 18 I think, perhaps too smugly, “ERM is just mean training loss.” I can read the average and the argmin. I mostly ignore the narrow box defining an empirical distribution with delta functions: I cannot yet connect that box to a computation I need. Slide 19's zero-one loss is easy to implement but raises a real question: how are we minimizing that with gradients? I assume there will be a distinction between the score we care about and a training loss. It is not answered here. On slide 20 I slow down. Instead of directly mapping features to a label, we can describe features within each class and use Bayes to reverse the question. I can imagine estimating a mean and covariance for each species. I have heard of that, but putting it next to the pair plot helps it become a model rather than a classifier name.

There is also a quiet confusion here: the opening posterior was about the unknown parameter; this posterior is about the class with theta still on the conditioning side. I know those are different objects if I stop and read, but in real time “posterior” has one bucket in my head. I do not yet have a clean account of whether the classifier's parameter uncertainty has been handled. Slide 21 calling training misclassification rate “performance” bothers me a little. Surely we care about unseen patients, not the rows we fitted. I wait rather than assuming the lecturer has forgotten testing.

When the taxonomies arrive on slides 24–27, my first response is that I already know supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning. The formulas on slide 25 do not all become executable stories. I follow prediction and recognize generation, but I do not decode the full action expression. The table on slide 26 does something more useful: next-token prediction and language generation can be different tasks supported by the same self-supervised experience. So these are two axes, not competing names for kinds of network. That corrects my usual habit of calling an LLM “a generative model” as though that says how its training examples were obtained.

I still wonder what the dashes mean. There is a dash for supervised generation, but generating a requested class of image sounds as if labels could be useful. I would be willing to ask that aloud. “Common pairings” sounds like examples; “not every type of experience supports every task” makes the table sound more restrictive. I do not know which reading to take into an exam.

Slides 29–34 make the distinction less semantic. The same MNIST array can be used to predict a digit, form a representation, or produce another digit image. The file on disk does not determine the problem. I begin thinking about our old ECG files: predicting an annotation and learning a representation of the waveform are different requests even if the loader starts with the same files. That is my own transfer, not a biomedical example supplied by the lecture. The input shapes on slides 30–31 are effortless to read. The train/test arrow on slide 31 also answers my earlier worry in principle, although it does not explain the assumptions behind a valid split. I still do not understand my old cross-validation practice any more deeply.

The repeated prediction/discovery/generation panels start to blur by IMDB. I know roughly what an embedding is, so I am probably granting myself too much credit for understanding the discovery variable z. I cannot say from these slides how its performance function would be chosen. I retain the next-token conditional much better. Slide 35 then feels like a turn: now all this is becoming a joint distribution and three operations. It is called a summary, but I am not sure I could derive that joint distribution from any of the preceding examples. I understand the proposed destination more than the connection.

The Gaussian section brings me back. On slide 37 I recognize the quadratic form and check that the mean is a vector and the covariance a square matrix. The figures on slide 38 are especially good for me. Marginalizing means collecting over the coordinate we are not using; conditioning means looking at a particular observed level and normalizing that slice. Those are different array operations in my head, and the shifted narrow curve makes the difference visible. I would sketch that figure rather than copy every integral.

Covariance and correlation on slides 39–40 are mostly familiar from signals. I like the reminder about units. I briefly map zero covariance to independence automatically, then the explicit warning on slide 39 catches me. Slide 41's covariance proof is one I actually follow: center, apply A, take the outer product, move fixed matrices outside the expectation. Dimensions help me more than prose here. The Mahalanobis slide makes the inverse covariance less mysterious: distance in the cloud's own units. The three contour pictures on slide 43 consolidate that. This does not feel like useless formalism.

On slide 46 I mentally make two index lists for the observed and unobserved coordinates. The marginal rule on slide 47 sounds wonderfully simple: select the matching mean and covariance blocks. But the displayed proof appears to multiply `[I 0]` by a block row `[Sigma_11 Sigma_12]` and then by a block column. I cannot make those dimensions work. I assume something has been omitted and keep the result, but for a moment I am debugging the slide instead of following it.

Slides 48–49 require more effort. I understand the conditional mean as a baseline plus a matrix multiplying the observed deviation. I recognize the residual argument as related to regression, but I would not confidently reproduce every step after one pass. The statement that zero cross-covariance gives independence is now explicitly tied to a joint Gaussian, which helps resolve slide 39 rather than contradict it. What really surprises me is that the conditional covariance depends on which variables I observe, not their measured values. A wildly surprising measurement changes the mean but gives the same covariance under this model? I would put a question mark next to that and test it.

The scalar example on slides 50–51 is where I catch up completely: correlation 0.8, observation 1.5, updated mean 1.2, variance 0.36. I can verify those numbers. Slide 52 gives the leftover covariance a name, the Schur complement. I would write the name beside the subtraction formula, partly because orange bold mathematics looks like exam material. I understand it as the uncertainty left after the predictable part has been removed. I would be bluffing if I claimed I had mastered block elimination. Slide 53's separation of the quadratic is plausible to me, but my attention drops; I accept it rather than check it. The summary on slide 54 is useful recovery time.

“State and measurement” on slide 56 is immediately my language. Here is a model I can simulate: draw z, multiply by W, add an offset and noise. I particularly appreciate “the parameters are given.” Otherwise I would be asking whether W is fitted, calibrated, or a network. The graph from the beginning has become useful again. I also notice that this is inference in a supplied model, whereas much of the first half was about training one. I can see why inference belongs in the course, but the boundary between those activities is still something I am sorting out.

The block covariance on slide 58 is small enough that I concentrate on the familiar `W Sigma W^T + Omega` part first. Slides 59–60 show that the frightening conditional formula is a prediction-error correction. I think, “This looks like the Kalman update.” I would not claim the lecture has given us a complete Kalman filter: there is no time evolution here. But seeing the gain come from cross-covariance and measurement covariance rather than being a smoothing parameter somebody chose is satisfying. The scalar reduction and three cases on slides 61–62 are what I will remember. Larger measurement noise means less correction; greater prior uncertainty means more correction. That is an operational distinction.

Slide 63 makes me revise another shortcut. I have been casually calling the updated mean “the posterior.” The slide separates the whole conditional distribution from the number extracted from it. I knew those words before, but the two-step picture forces me to notice what my implementation usually throws away.

Sensor fusion on slide 64 is probably my favorite slide. In the directional example the estimate is not simply a scalar weighted point along the line connecting the two measurements. One sensor can be more useful in one direction and the other in another direction. That makes the covariance matrix matter in a way that “confidence score” never did. At the same time, I ask where the prior shown at the top went in the picture. If the prior is very strong somewhere else, why must equal sensor uncertainty put the posterior mean between the measurements? And if two sensors share a motion artifact, am I allowed to combine them this way? Those are implementation questions I would actually ask, not requests for another taxonomy.

Slide 65 closes the loop: the same model can generate a distribution of measurements before there is an observed measurement to condition on. I can now separate simulating possible sensor output from inferring a hidden state from actual output. At slide 67 I retain the four anchors unevenly. The dataset/task distinction is clear; Gaussian conditioning and sensor fusion are useful; the three probability operations are much clearer now than when their first summary appeared. I am tired, and I could not redo all the proofs, but I no longer think this was only introductory vocabulary.

## 3. What Amir would do next

I would first write a small NumPy simulation of the slide 51 bivariate example. Generate correlated pairs, compare the marginal first-coordinate histogram with a narrow band around the observed second coordinate, and check the conditional mean and variance. Then change the observed value without changing the covariance. I want to see the cloud move without its conditional width changing. I know that selecting a finite band only approximates conditioning on an exact value, so I would check sensitivity to the band width rather than expect identical numbers.

Next I would implement the linear measurement update with a two-dimensional state and two measurements. I would plot the ellipses and the updated mean, reproduce the directional sensor-fusion picture, and vary the prior strength. I would also try correlated measurement noise, because that is closer to the problems I remember from real recordings. I would look up the relation to the Kalman measurement update and how the covariance inputs are estimated or calibrated. The lecture gives those parameters to me; it has not taught me to obtain reliable values for them.

The terminology I can now use with some substance is empirical risk, marginalization, conditioning, covariance blocks, and gain. I can distinguish a posterior distribution from its mean when I stop to think. I would probably say “Schur complement” confidently while still needing the formula open beside me. I might also casually say “generative means unsupervised” if I relied on the table more than the examples; that is not settled in my head. “Discovery” still mostly means embeddings or PCA to me, rather than a precisely specified task and criterion.

My actual notes would be sparse: the Beta count update, the two taxonomy axes, the three-operation sketch, `A Sigma A^T`, the conditional Gaussian formulas, the numerical 1.2/0.36 example, and the gain equation with the three noise regimes. I would not copy the historical dates, the full task-taxonomy formulas, or the empirical-distribution box. I would save the slide 64 picture and write “prior? shared noise?” next to it.

## 4. Analyst's assessment

Amir's experience does not support calling the opening conceptual work pointless. His initial impatience is mainly temperamental and reflects familiarity with software interfaces. The distinction between an experience regime and an output task genuinely changes his understanding by slides 26–32. The same-data argument becomes stronger again in slides 56–65, where changing the probabilistic query changes what is computed. His first dismissal is corrected by the lecture's later material.

The mathematical difficulty is also partly productive. Slides 41 and 46–51 reward his ability to reason about dimensions, transformations, and residuals. He does not need every formula replaced by code. The progression from the general conditioning rule to an actual scalar calculation and then a measurement update allows an initially effortful expression to become meaningful. The figures on slides 38, 43, 62, and 64 carry different kinds of understanding; they are not interchangeable decoration. In particular, the anisotropic sensor example gives him a reason to care about full covariance rather than a single uncertainty number.

There are genuine presentational and mathematical problems in the rendered lecture. The expanded product in the marginal proof on slide 47 contains a block row where the full covariance matrix would be required if the leading selector is retained. As displayed, it is not a conformable matrix product. This is particularly disruptive for a student using dimensions as a trustworthy way into the algebra. It is separate from his ordinary difficulty with conditional notation.

The sensor-fusion statements on slide 64 also leave material assumptions unstated. A prior is displayed, but the geometric conclusions do not explain its influence. Equal measurement uncertainties alone do not ensure that a posterior mean lies between the two measurements in the presence of an arbitrary informative prior. The two displayed observation conditionals do not by themselves specify the dependence between measurement noises either. Amir's questions about a strong prior and shared artifacts expose missing conditions in the presented example, not merely a reluctance to accept an idealization.

The dashes in slide 26 create a real ambiguity between a table of common examples and a table of permitted combinations. The accompanying statement about unsupported pairings strengthens the restrictive reading. Amir's conditional-generation question is a sensible test of that ambiguity. His informal prior habit of treating “generative” and “unsupervised” as near-synonyms is partly corrected by separating the axes, but the table can also preserve it.

Other gaps are more appropriately described as incomplete connections than errors. Slides 14–15 introduce several function symbols before Amir has a settled mapping for them. Slides 25 and 33 compress heterogeneous task and performance specifications into small panels; he can recognize their labels without understanding their formulas. Slide 35's joint-distribution summary arrives before he feels that the examples have earned that common representation. Later Gaussian examples repair much of this, but he retains the abstract task formulas only shallowly. Small mathematical blocks on slides 58 and 61 further favor recognition over independent reconstruction, although the larger update interpretation in slides 60 and 62 restores the main point.

Training performance versus performance on future cases is mentioned across slides 18–21, 31, and 33, but not developed into an account of validation. Amir's existing skepticism makes him notice that boundary. It would be inaccurate to report that the lecture teaches him how to validate his old classifier, and equally inaccurate to treat the absence of a full validation lesson as proof that the lecture confuses training and test data. The concrete unresolved issue is how the early performance criterion relates to the future-case expectation.

Several of Amir's remaining misconceptions belong primarily to him. He initially over-identifies a probabilistic graph with a program flowchart, a posterior with its mean, and familiarity with an embedding API with understanding a discovery criterion. Slide 63 explicitly corrects the posterior/mean shortcut. Slides 39 and 48 jointly correct his automatic association of zero covariance with independence by identifying the Gaussian condition. His weak retention of the Schur-complement derivation is not evidence that the derivation has no place: he retains its residual-uncertainty interpretation and has a concrete reason to revisit it.

The broad belief that observations always reduce uncertainty is more mixed. The opening Beta example on slide 8 demonstrates a narrower posterior in that instance, and the later Gaussian conditioning results legitimately make strong variance-reduction statements within their setting. Amir is likely to merge those into a universal rule. The lecture's local claims and the learner's generalization should be distinguished; the review does not attribute an explicit universal theorem to the slides.

By the end, Amir understands substantially more than he could derive unaided. His most useful transfer is from a pipeline that returns a point prediction to a model whose conditional distribution supports several different outputs and retains uncertainty. His most important remaining limitation is that he can implement the supplied Gaussian update more readily than he can justify the supplied model and covariance assumptions. That is an honest boundary of this lecture experience, not a failure of interest.
