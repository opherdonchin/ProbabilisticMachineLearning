## 1. Intellectual arc

A black-and-white image contains local patterns: adjacent pixels often agree. If a few pixels are corrupted, treating each observed pixel in isolation wastes that information. This lecture builds a probability model that combines the noisy measurement with a preference for neighboring pixels to agree. Students then improve a candidate clean image one pixel at a time. The method is easy to explain and check, although it can stop at an imperfect answer.

Students bring Bayes' rule, Gaussian likelihoods and basic graph familiarity. They do not need advanced sampling theory. Five teaching responsibilities preserve the existing budget:

1. **What a graph says about a probability model — 14 minutes.** Recall a directed graph such as two independent causes pointing to a shared effect. Explain that conditioning on the shared effect can make the causes dependent. A graph can guarantee an independence; an existing edge does not prove dependence for every choice of numerical parameters. An arrow alone does not establish a causal claim. End by summarizing these limits.
2. **Build a model from neighboring-pixel preferences — 22 minutes.** Represent a pixel by −1 or +1. Give matching neighbors a higher score. Multiplying these local scores gives an overall score, not yet probabilities that sum to one. Compute the required normalizing constant on four pixels. This is an *Ising model*: a binary model with pairwise interactions. An undirected graphical model, also called a *Markov random field*, records those interactions without choosing a parent direction. End by summarizing local scores versus a normalized joint distribution.
3. **Hold the other pixels fixed and examine one pixel — 27 minutes.** Derive its probability from just its neighbors. All factors unrelated to that pixel cancel from the ratio, including the global normalizer. Explain why this conditional probability is different from a pixel's probability when the other pixels are unknown. State the independence guaranteed by separating nodes in the graph. Briefly compare the shielding set in an undirected graph (neighbors) with a directed graph (parents, children and the other parents of those children). This set is called a Markov blanket. Simply erasing arrows does not preserve these independence rules. End by summarizing what can be calculated locally.
4. **Add noisy measurements and improve a candidate image — 21 minutes.** Use a Gaussian measurement model with explicit variance. Derive the contribution of the observed pixel and combine it with its neighbors. Change a pixel only when this increases the posterior score, keeping it unchanged on a tie. Show a four-pixel example where no single change helps but changing all pixels would help. This method is called *coordinate maximization* or *iterated conditional modes* (ICM). It produces one candidate image, not samples or a complete uncertainty calculation. End by summarizing both the improvement property and the failure to guarantee the best whole image.
5. **Show exactly which factors use which variables — 16 minutes.** Draw variables and the local score functions separately. This *factor graph* is a diagram of a product of functions. Reuse the image model and briefly represent a familiar directed chain in the same way. Name the multicolor extension, called the Potts model, without a second calculation. An optional five-minute Gaussian comparison connects graph edges with L01 precision matrices. End with a retrospective summary of the model and local calculation.

## 2. Mathematical spine

The central calculation runs from a product of local scores to one conditional probability, then to one improving pixel update. Here `z_i` is a clean pixel, `N(i)` its neighbors, `J_ij` an agreement strength and `h_i` a preference for one sign. Each undirected edge is counted once.

| Mathematical commitment | What to explain and how deeply |
|---|---|
| Directed factorization `p(x)=∏_i p(x_i|x_pa(i))` | Apply familiar chain/fork/shared-effect examples. State the conditioned-descendant caveat. Graphical separation guarantees conditional independence; an unblocked path permits dependence but does not force it. The formal inclusion is `I(G)⊆I(p)`, with `I` denoting sets of independence statements. No full graph-search algorithm. |
| Product model `p(z)=Z⁻¹∏_c ψ_c(z_c)`, `Z=Σ_z ∏_c ψ_c(z_c)` | State and work on four pixels. A potential `ψ` is a nonnegative compatibility score; its variables form a fully connected subset, called a clique. Explain clique versus maximal clique. Scores are not individual conditional probabilities. |
| Score `S(z)=Σ_i h_i z_i+Σ_(i,j) J_ij z_i z_j`; energy `E(z)=−S(z)` | Work the model and derive `p∝exp(−E)` by taking logarithms. For general strictly positive factors, the product becomes an additive energy: `E(z)=−Σ_c log ψ_c(z_c)`. Energy need not be positive and can shift by a constant. Apply L02's exponential-family form and `A=log Z`, without another moment derivation. |
| Four-cycle normalizer `Z(J)=2e^(4J)+12+2e^(−4J)` | Work the 16 states grouped by score. Brute-force enumeration grows as `2^n`; this is not a claim that every graph necessarily requires exponential computation. |
| A separating set blocks information; a node is independent of nonneighbors given its neighbors | State and work on the exact four-cycle below; also state that two nonneighbors are independent when all remaining nodes are fixed. For strictly positive distributions, graph independence and clique factorization are equivalent (the Hammersley–Clifford theorem); state the qualification, no proof. Under positivity the graph-wide separation, neighbor and pairwise formulations agree. Distinguish guaranteed graph independences from additional numerical ones. |
| `p(z_i|z_−i)∝∏_(c:i∈c) ψ_c(z_c)` | Derive cancellation carefully. Here `z_−i` means all other pixels fixed. |
| Local field `H_i=h_i+Σ_(j∈N(i))J_ij z_j`; `p(z_i=+1|z_−i)=1/(1+e^(−2H_i))` | Derive two-state normalization and log odds `2H_i`; work one value. “Field” means the combined preference for that pixel. This is conditional, not marginal probability. |
| `y_i|z_i~N(z_i,σ²)`; posterior score `S(z;y)=Σ_edges J_ij z_i z_j+Σ_i(h_i+y_i/σ²)z_i+constant` | Derive by expanding the Gaussian square and using `z_i²=1`. Standard deviation is `σ`; variance is `σ²`. The posterior has its own normalizer. Parameters are known here. |
| Flip score change `ΔS=−2z_iH_i(y)`; update to the sign of `H_i(y)` | Derive why an actual sequential change improves the score. Keep current sign at zero. A finite state space then gives termination if updates run until none improve. A sweep limit only records improvements so far. Work the local-stop failure example. |
| Factor graph `f(z)=∏_a f_a(z_scope(a))` | State and work variable–function connections. A factor may use more than two variables. The drawing is not an ordinary variable-only independence graph; no message-passing derivation. |
| Potts score `exp(J_P 1[k=l])`; optional Gaussian `Λ_ij=0 ⇒ X_i⟂X_j|X_−ij` | Potts: name only; in the binary case `J_P=2J_Ising` up to a constant. Gaussian: optional five-minute application of the L01 quadratic form. |

## 3. Notebook handoff — restore an image with a checked local calculation

The lecture establishes the full probability model and update mathematics. The lab expands a hand-calculated square to a small, course-created black-and-white geometric image. Students implement the local preference/probability calculation, check it against all 16 states of the square, and run supplied one-pixel-at-a-time updates. They compare spatial restoration with treating each observed pixel alone (`J=0`). They also run the tiny bad-start example so the algorithm's limitation is visible.

**Students write:** the local field and conditional probability, their numerical check, and interpretation of score/reconstruction comparisons. **Supplied:** image/noise generation, fixed seed, open-boundary neighbor handling, state enumeration, plotting, stopping records and sequential sweep driver. Exact larger-image size and settings belong to later worked-solution authoring. NumPy/Matplotlib suffice; a stable sigmoid helper may be supplied. No new inference library or sampling implementation.

Allocate 10 minutes to tiny-model normalization, 15 to local probability and variance scaling, 20 to restoration and score traces, 10 to a bounded coupling/noise comparison and failure example, and 5 to interpretation. Lower pixel error is illustrative, not guaranteed for every setting. If coding takes too long, supply more helper code before removing interpretation.

**What is lost by the simpler method:** students obtain one improved image. They do not estimate the probability of each pixel after averaging over other possible images, draw posterior samples, or certify the globally most probable image. Later L08 teaches exact calculations on suitable structured models, L09 approximate distributions and L10 sampling. None must repeat the restoration lab.

No notebook runtime or Colab readiness is claimed. A validated worked solution, student timing trial, clean local uv run and hosted Colab run are later obligations.

## 4. Concrete examples and source checks

All numbered examples below are course-created unless stated otherwise; Murphy supplies model principles, not these exact numbers.

- **Shared effect:** `A→C←B`, independent fair binary `A,B`; `p(C=1|A=B=0)=0.1`, otherwise `0.9`. Conditioning on `C=1` gives `p(A=1)=9/14` and `p(A=B=1)=9/28`, unlike `(9/14)²`. An optional child `C→D` illustrates why conditioning on a descendant can also open the path; no numerical model for D is promised.
- **Four-pixel square:** top row 1,2; bottom row 3,4. Edges are exactly `{(1,2),(1,3),(2,4),(3,4)}`, with no diagonals or wrapping. Zero unary preferences and common J give score sums +4 (2 states), 0 (12) and −4 (2). At `J=0.5`, `Z≈27.0487827643`; at zero, `Z=16`. Pixels 1 and 4 are conditionally independent given 2 and 3; conditioning on 2 alone does not separate them. Grouping and edges were checked by 16-state arithmetic.
- **Bad stopping point:** same square, `J=0.5`, `h=0`, `σ²=1`, every observed `y_i=0.2`. Starting all-negative gives each field −0.8 and score 1.2, so every single-pixel update stays negative. All-positive has score 2.8 and is the globally best state, checked across 16 states. This directly demonstrates the limitation.
- **Optional Gaussian connection:** precision `Λ=[[2,−1,0],[−1,2,−1],[0,−1,2]]`, covariance `Σ=(1/4)[[3,2,1],[2,4,2],[1,2,3]]`. Variables 1 and 3 correlate marginally but become independent given 2. Leading principal minors 2,3,4 establish positive definiteness.
