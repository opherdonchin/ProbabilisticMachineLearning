Proceed with the proposed four-file patch, but expand the experiment to address **slide density and visual layout**, not only mathematical completeness and presentation clarity.

The objective is to develop a workflow that reliably produces mathematically substantive slides that are easy to follow during a live graduate lecture.

### 1. Revise the proposed instructions

Address three separate responsibilities:

**Mathematical selection (Design)**
- Decide which mathematical statements, calculations, assumptions, and inferential warrants students must see.
- Distinguish essential visible reasoning from established background, routine algebra, and technical checking.
- Preserve enough reasoning for students to understand how the conclusion follows, not merely verify that it is mathematically correct.

**Visual density and hierarchy (Design and Authoring)**
- Assess how much students must simultaneously read, interpret, and relate.
- Ensure that the central mathematical object or result is visually identifiable.
- Arrange equations, visual objects, local explanatory cues, and key points so their relationships are evident.
- Avoid competing blocks of similar visual weight, unnecessary labels, excessive fragmentation, and stacks of equations whose logical relationships are unclear.
- Judge both spatial density and cognitive density. A slide that technically fits may still be crowded or difficult to understand.
- Use the established course slides as concrete precedents for mathematical scale, spacing, hierarchy, and object/key-point roles.
- Do not resolve density by shrinking text, removing essential explanations, or hiding too much behind reveals.

**Negotiation across stages (Organization, Design, Authoring)**
- If the proposed mathematics cannot be presented clearly at normal slide scale, consider reorganizing, selectively simplifying, changing spatial composition, or splitting the teaching move across slides.
- Authoring should offer concrete layout alternatives and evidence, rather than merely report overflow or reduce font sizes.
- Design should reconsider the content and semantic grouping when implementation demonstrates excessive density.
- Organization should reconsider slide boundaries or count when the argument cannot be communicated well within its provisional allocation.
- Do not treat a provisional slide count or layout as an instructor commitment. Preserve approved mathematical depth and lecture scope.

Make targeted changes to the existing design conventions, Slide Design skill, Slide Authoring skill, and shared convergence protocol. Modify Slide Organization instructions only if the existing negotiation contract is insufficient.

### 2. Conduct a controlled experiment

Apply the revised instructions in an isolated worktree, freezing their versions before testing.

Use fresh specialists on two contrasting examples:

**L01 slides 64–65: squared-error optimality**
- This is the principal density and clarity test.
- The mathematical derivation must remain understandable, but not every step or qualification must be projected.
- Allow a genuine comparison of different layouts and, if justified, different slide divisions.
- Evaluate whether a student can follow the progression from the loss to the conditional-risk decomposition and optimality result.
- Inspect how explanatory cues, equations, and interpretation work together.

**L01 slide 48: Gaussian independence**
- This tests whether selective simplification preserves essential mathematical justification.
- Gaussianity's role in converting zero covariance into independence must remain clear.
- The slide should retain an appropriate mathematical argument without unnecessary density.

Supply the actual preceding and following slides and relevant strong precedents from the lecture. Do not prescribe the desired layouts, omissions, or slide counts in the task briefs.

### 3. Evaluate the actual rendered results

Use an independent reviewer who initially sees only the rendered slides and their neighbors, without the authors' explanations or expected defect list.

Evaluate separately:

1. Mathematical validity and sufficient visible reasoning.
2. Pedagogical clarity: can the argument be reconstructed from what students see?
3. Visual density: is the amount of simultaneously visible material reasonable at actual presentation scale?
4. Layout clarity: are hierarchy, grouping, alignment, spacing, and relationships between mathematical elements effective?
5. Reveal logic: does each step direct attention appropriately without disrupting continuity?
6. Consistency with the established course visual language.

Inspect the final handout and every meaningful reveal state at actual slide scale. Compilation, absence of clipping, and correct equations are necessary but not sufficient.

Compare the results with existing slides. In particular, do not reward an output simply for containing less text, fewer equations, or more whitespace. The goal is **greater comprehensibility without sacrificing necessary mathematics**.

- Any tradeoffs between two-slide and three-slide arrangements.
- Specific evidence of where the revised instructions succeeded or failed.
- A recommendation on adopting, revising, or rejecting the instruction patch.
