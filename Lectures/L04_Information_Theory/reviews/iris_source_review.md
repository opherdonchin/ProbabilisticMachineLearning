Murphy §5.1.6, **KL divergence and MLE**, asks why likelihood fitting is a distribution-matching criterion. For a finite categorical empirical reference, $D_{KL}(\widehat p\Vert q)= -N^{-1}\sum_n\log q(x_n)-H(\widehat p)$, so minimization equals MLE. The next substantive consequence is overfitting: the empirical training distribution need not equal the population distribution. The source's Dirac-atom calculation for a continuous empirical measure against a density is formal, because literal measure-theoretic KL there is infinite; a finite categorical illustration avoids that issue.

§5.2.4, **Cross entropy and perplexity**, gives the population identity $H_{ce}(p,q)=H(p)+D_{KL}(p\Vert q)$. Entropy is the irreducible reference uncertainty and the KL term measures the excess incurred by the model. The sample average negative log likelihood estimates cross entropy; perplexity is its exponential in the matching log base. The bigram return interprets it as an effective branching factor. This transition supports model assessment as well as training; it does not make a held-out average an exactly known population KL.

§§5.3.1–5.3.2, **Definition / Interpretation**, turn the comparison of distributions into dependence: $I(X;Y)=D_{KL}(p_{XY}\Vert p_Xp_Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)$. The joint law must be specified or estimated before the quantity is computable. Independence is exactly zero MI; the entropy reduction is averaged over the conditioning variable, not a claim about every conditioning outcome.

§5.3.3, **Data processing inequality**, asks whether transforming noisy observations can increase information about an unknown source. For $X\to Y\to Z$, conditional independence makes $I(X;Z\mid Y)=0$; the two chain-rule expansions and nonnegative conditional MI establish $I(X;Z)\le I(X;Y)$. §5.3.4, **Sufficient statistics**, then identifies equality for $\theta\to X\to s(X)$ as task-relevant information preservation. This connects naturally to L02's likelihood sufficient statistics. Information preservation is relative to the specified target/joint law; it does not imply that an arbitrary representation, fitted predictor or finite test score is sufficient.

§5.5, **Error-correcting codes (channel coding)**, supplies a concrete return to graphical modeling: two uniform message bits, a deterministic even-parity bit, and independent noisy observations form a factored joint. For received `100` and flip probability $0.2$, valid codewords `000`, `110`, `101` are tied posterior modes and `011` is less likely. The example exposes ambiguity under a noisy posterior and the distinction between redundancy, error detection and correction. Its broad channel-coding motivation does not give this distance-two code guaranteed single-error correction. Source §5.5 is a complementary concrete inference example, not the theorem that derives predictive log scoring or DPI.

§14.2.1, **Proper scoring rules**, is the focused additional route when evaluating conditional predictions. With the true law $p^*(x,y)$, $E[-\log q(Y\mid X)]=H_{p^*}(Y\mid X)+E_{p^*(X)}D_{KL}(p^*(Y\mid X)\Vert q(Y\mid X))$. The expected log score is uniquely optimal at the true conditional distribution, up to $p^*(X)$-null sets; with finite labels and adequate support this is strict propriety. A finite sample score estimates that expectation and cannot certify probability truth or calibration. The source calls the equality-only property “proper”; standard terminology distinguishes proper from strictly proper.

**Relevant source corrections:** §5.2.4's final lower bound prints $2^{-H(p^*)}$; it should be $2^{H(p^*)}$. Its “second order” label for $p(x_n\mid x_{n-1})$ should be first order. §14.2.1's uniqueness condition should be called strict propriety and qualified almost everywhere.
