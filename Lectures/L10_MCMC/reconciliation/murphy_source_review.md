## Independently reconstructed source argument

Chapter 11 starts with a **numerical query**, not a posterior-family competition: compute `mu=E_p[f(X)]`. Low-dimensional quadrature loses scalability; independent sample averages replace spatial coverage by sampling where probability lies. The `Var(f)/S` variance calculation needs independent draws and finite variance. The apparently dimension-free rate does not remove dimension-dependent sampling cost or an unfavorable query variance. The source immediately names those two catches (§§11.2–11.2.2, pp.483–485). A histogram or kernel density estimate is a display, not the Monte Carlo estimator.

The next question is **how to obtain samples**. Simple random-generation mechanisms lead to rejection and importance sampling. In §§11.5.1–11.5.3, pp.491–492, the key move is to sample a proposal and change its contribution to the expectation by a density ratio. Self-normalization removes the need for the normalizer but creates a finite-sample biased ratio estimator. A learned VI distribution can be an importance proposal (§11.5.3); support coverage alone does not ensure manageable weight variance. Thus an optional VI connection is faithful, but reverse-KL fitting cannot be advertised as automatically repairing its own missed tails by reweighting. The query function matters to proposal quality, not only the target density.

Variance reduction is a second lever, separate from generating more draws. In §11.6.2, pp.494–495, replace `f(X,Y)` by `h(X)=E[f(X,Y)|X]` when that conditional integration is tractable. The tower property preserves the expectation; total variance proves

$$\operatorname{Var}(h(X))=\operatorname{Var}(f(X,Y))-E[\operatorname{Var}(f(X,Y)\mid X)]\leq\operatorname{Var}(f(X,Y)).$$

This is a per-draw variance result; with equally many IID draws it also orders average variances. It is **not by itself** a theorem ordering the cost or asymptotic variance of two different dependent Markov chains. This distinction matters when the later collapsed-Gibbs prose appeals to Rao–Blackwellization.

Chapter 12 introduces a different sampling mechanism for high-dimensional targets: **construct a Markov transition that preserves the target**, then average a dependent sequence (§12.1, p.499). MH allows user-designed proposals plus a correction; the normalizer cancels, and rejected states must be retained. Detailed balance establishes invariance, with extra chain conditions needed for limiting behavior (§§12.2.1–12.2.2, pp.500–502). These are valid ingredients for a bounded bridge, not evidence that undergraduates need a second full introductory lecture. Murphy's one-step positive-proposal condition (12.5) is sufficient and stronger than necessary; local/block moves need not jump to every target state in one step.

The **structure branch** in §12.3 is substantial ML mathematics. Full conditional draws exploit factorization; the GMM progression explicitly distinguishes an illustrative known-parameter generation task (§12.3.5.1, p.509) from learning unknown parameters via their joint posterior (§12.3.5.2, p.510). Calling both examples 'Bayesian clustering' would hide a change of target. Single-site Gibbs can move slowly when conditional spread is small relative to marginal spread (§12.3.7, p.511). Blocking allows a tractable *joint* conditional move. Murphy's explicit example alternates unknown HMM parameters with the whole hidden path, using conjugacy for the former and forward-filtering backward-sampling for the latter. This source argument is not just 'Gibbs is MH with acceptance one': exact internal subproblems can be building blocks of an overall approximate Monte Carlo calculation.

Collapsing is a **different structural intervention**, not another name for blocking (§12.3.8, pp.512–513). Integrate out continuous GMM parameters, then sample assignment indicators using posterior predictive probabilities. Marginalization reduces the variables sampled but introduces dependencies among the remaining variables. Cached sufficient statistics make conditional updates feasible. The source states both naive and collapsed variants have `O(NKD)` sweep cost in its stated setting; it does not establish a universal lower big-O cost or mixing guarantee for collapse. Its principal stated extension is to unbounded-component mixtures, not an obligation to teach Dirichlet processes. A fully conjugate mixture is required for the analytic integration: the independent mean/covariance prior in §12.3.5.2 should not silently be treated as the normal–inverse-Wishart joint-conjugate prior needed by a standard collapsed Gaussian-mixture derivation.

Auxiliary methods (§12.4, p.513 onward) make the complementary move: **add** variables to make transitions easier, then discard them. This prepares HMC's argument. At §12.5, p.517, Murphy identifies local random perturbation as a cause of inefficient high-dimensional search, then uses gradients plus momentum to make sustained moves through a continuous target. Energy-conserving Hamiltonian flow and volume preservation explain why a coherent proposal can preserve a distribution; leapfrog supplies a reversible, volume-preserving numerical construction, and MH corrects its finite integration error (§§12.5.1–12.5.3, pp.517–520). A momentum reversal or equivalent reversibility argument belongs in a mathematically correct formulation. NUTS/tuning are further machinery, not free consequences of knowing the target gradient.

This is an argument about **geometry and computation**, not a promise to cross isolated modes. Even exact Hamiltonian motion at total energy `h` cannot reach potential energy greater than `h`; momentum refresh changes that energy but does not establish frequent barrier crossing. Nor does a fixed mass matrix remove changing local scales in a funnel. Murphy returns to those difficulties in §§12.5.4 and 12.6.5 rather than declaring HMC a universal solution.

The ML-specific elaboration remains concrete. §12.5.8, pp.523–524, integrates **discrete** assignments out to permit continuous-gradient HMC, the opposite variable elimination from collapsed Gibbs. For an HMM it names the forward algorithm for the marginal likelihood. It also requires unconstrained continuous coordinates. A density in transformed coordinates needs the change-of-variables Jacobian; 'take logits' is not a complete density specification, and K unconstrained logits for a K-category simplex have a redundant additive direction unless a proper identifiable parameterization is used. §12.5.9 identifies a second bottleneck: the full-data energy/gradient sum costs `O(N)`.

Convergence and useful finite accuracy are an explicit separate responsibility (§12.6, pp.524–533). A stationary law is not finite-run adequacy; diagnostic heuristics cannot establish that all relevant regions were visited. With stationary draws `F_s=f(X_s)`, finite second moment and suitable covariance summability/CLT assumptions, the large-run average variance is approximately `Var_p(f) tau_f/S`, where `tau_f=1+2 sum_{k>=1} corr(F_0,F_k)`. ESS is query-dependent, and negative autocorrelation can give ESS greater than S. Murphy's blanket prose that dependent samples have higher variance needs this qualification. A small estimated MCSE cannot represent an unseen mode or a stuck-chain transient.

The **large-data branch** (§12.7, pp.533–536) starts from a product likelihood and derives a per-step bottleneck. SGLD replaces the full gradient by an unbiased minibatch estimator and deliberately adds calibrated Gaussian noise. Later preconditioning, variance reduction and momentum methods address distinct costs or geometry; they are not required to understand the initial tradeoff. The SGD diffusion analogy (§12.5.7) does not imply ordinary minibatch SGD samples the desired posterior: its noise is generally state-dependent, anisotropic and differently scaled. This is a coherent separate source argument, not an excuse to collect HMC, SGLD, SGHMC and NUTS into one methods survey.

## Source defects and mathematical cautions that affect teaching

1. **Momentum covariance versus inverse mass, §§12.5.1–12.5.4.** Equation 12.64 uses `K(v)=v^T Sigma^{-1}v/2`, and Algorithm 12.3 draws `v~N(0,Sigma)` and moves position by `eta Sigma^{-1}v`. These agree with Sigma being the **mass/momentum covariance**. But the prose calls Sigma inverse mass; Eqs.12.75–12.76 instead print `v^T Sigma v/2`, and §12.5.4.3 recommends setting Sigma to posterior covariance. One cannot consistently reproduce these formulas together. Use an explicit convention `r~N(0,M)`, `K=r^TM^{-1}r/2`, `dot theta=M^{-1}r`. For a Gaussian target covariance C, `ddot theta=-M^{-1}C^{-1}theta`; choosing `M=C^{-1}` equalizes frequencies. Under that convention **inverse mass** is `C`, not mass. Explain rather than quietly swapping labels.
2. **Integrator/error prose.** Eq.12.69 substitutes the new momentum into what the preceding simultaneous Euler definition says is the old momentum. More importantly, §12.5.3's language of rejecting whenever energy changes, followed by accepting only if energy is constant, is misleading. Ordinary finite leapfrog error is precisely what `min(1,exp(-Delta H))` corrects; every nonzero energy error is not a divergence. Large integration failures and routine MH rejection require distinction.
3. **Step size, §12.5.4.2.** 'Square root of the smallest marginal standard deviation' is not the quadratic stability scale. With identity mass, Gaussian variance eigenvalues lambda_i give angular frequencies `1/sqrt(lambda_i)`, and leapfrog stability requires `eta<2 sqrt(lambda_min(C))` for this quadratic system. Principal directions, variance and standard deviation must not be interchanged. This is a local/quadratic diagnostic, not a universal tuning theorem.
4. **Collapsed mixture, Eq.12.56.** With `pi~Dir(alpha/K,...,alpha/K)`, the conditional is `(N_{k,-i}+alpha/K)/(N-1+alpha)`. The printed final numerator uses alpha, inconsistent with the gamma ratio and Algorithm 12.2. Also `N_{k,-i}=N_k-1` applies only to the observation's current component; generally subtract `I(z_i=k)`.
5. **Rao–Blackwell variance, Eq.11.55.** Correct the conditional-variance nesting as in the displayed equation above. Do not derive a general collapsed-chain speed guarantee from this identity.
6. **SGD scaling, §§12.5.7/12.7.1.** Eq.12.84 defines a sum loss but Eq.12.85 uses an unscaled minibatch mean; the unbiasedness claim (12.88) fits the average-loss convention. For the posterior energy `U=-log p0-sum_n log likelihood_n`, the correct estimator is

   $$\widehat g(\theta)=-\nabla\log p_0(\theta)-\frac{N}{B}\sum_{n\in B_t}\nabla\log p(x_n\mid\theta).$$

   **Equation 12.122 is correct in the original PDF:** it reads `-(N/B)(sum grad loglik + (B/N) grad logprior)`. The outer minus applies to both terms, and the prior is scaled once. Extracted text obscured the opening parenthesis, and my initial message incorrectly called the prior sign an error. The rendered printed p.533/PDF p.567 disproves that claim; the coordinator and builder were notified immediately. Preserve this correction rather than attributing an extraction defect to Murphy. With `theta'=theta-eta_t ghat+sqrt(2 eta_t) epsilon`, conditional update covariance is `2 eta_t I+eta_t^2 Var(ghat|theta)`. Unbiased gradient drift therefore does **not** make the finite-step transition exact. A fixed step has discretization and gradient-noise error. Decreasing-step asymptotic statements need a specified schedule, regularity/ergodicity and an appropriate estimator; conventional Robbins–Monro conditions alone are not a universal guarantee for arbitrary targets. The inspected Murphy SGLD passage does not supply such a theorem. Further source/theorem checking is needed before promising asymptotic exactness.
7. **Variance-reduced snapshot, Algorithm 12.5 line 5, p.535.** The original PDF prints a sum of energies for `gtilde`, but line 7 adds it to gradient vectors. The snapshot must be `sum_n grad En(theta_tilde)`. This affects only an optional extension; it is not a reason to add variance-reduction theory to Route C.
8. **Neal's funnel, Eqs.12.112/12.115.** Under the course's variance convention, `x|nu~N(0,exp(nu))` corresponds to `x=z exp(nu/2)`, whereas the source later writes `x=z exp(nu)`. Choose explicit variance or standard-deviation notation. The existing Eight Schools probe already records this; it used no HMC.

The claimed defects above are **original-PDF-render verified**, not extraction-only: Eq.11.55 on p.495; Eq.12.56/count statement on p.513; Eq.12.64/prose on p.517 versus Eqs.12.75–12.76/prose on p.519 and Algorithm12.3 on p.520; Eq.12.69 on p.518; step-size prose on p.520; inverse-mass/covariance recommendation on p.521; sum/mean mismatch on pp.522–523; funnel on p.532; Algorithm12.5 on p.535. Eq.12.122 on p.533 was also rendered and is **not defective**. The simplex-identifiability, Jacobian and finite-run caveats are reviewer mathematical qualifications, not claims of an additional literal typesetting error.
