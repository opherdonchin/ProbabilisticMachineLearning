## 1. Intellectual arc

Suppose we want to predict a continuous measurement, a yes/no outcome or a count from explanatory variables. A straight-line predictor can help in each case, but its output must be translated into an appropriate probability distribution. This lecture puts those three models in one framework, then explains how a program calculates derivatives and uses them to learn weights. The lab predicts all three Iris species from flower measurements.

A *generalized linear model* (GLM) keeps a weighted sum of predictors but changes how that sum determines the response distribution. An *automatic differentiation* (AD) program calculates derivatives of its numerical operations using the chain rule. *Stochastic gradient descent* (SGD) repeatedly adjusts weights using a randomly selected subset of training observations. These are three linked responsibilities, not three assumed prerequisites.

1. **One framework for different response types — 30 minutes.** Carry L02's distribution form forward, now with predictors x. Compare Gaussian measurements, Bernoulli yes/no responses and Poisson counts. Explain the *link function*: it maps the predicted mean to the weighted sum. For counts the log mean is linear; for binary responses the log odds are linear; for a Gaussian response the mean itself is linear. Work one count prediction and explain the different response ranges and variances. Briefly distinguish a different valid link from the standard, or *canonical*, link. End by summarizing the common structure and differences.
2. **Which direction improves the probability of the data? — 20 minutes.** Minimize average negative log likelihood, the loss for assigning low probabilities to observed outcomes. Derive one common gradient: predicted-minus-observed response, multiplied by predictors and scaled by dispersion. A gradient is a direction/rate of change; the update rule still must choose a step. State the conditions and limits of convexity: a bowl-shaped loss need not have a finite, uniquely determined best coefficient. Optional prior-penalty recognition is brief. End by summarizing the gradient's meaning and sign.
3. **Predict one of three classes — 20 minutes.** Iris has three possible labels, so assign three probabilities instead of a single yes/no probability. Introduce *logits*, unconstrained numerical class scores, and *softmax*, which converts them to probabilities summing to one. Apply the same prediction-error idea to a vector of class probabilities and check matrix dimensions. A common shift of all scores changes no probability. End with the class probability, loss and gradient relationship.
4. **How a program obtains the derivative — 20 minutes.** Work one complete binary-loss calculation, first forward through elementary operations and then backward through their derivatives. When one intermediate value is used along two paths, add both contributions. This backward procedure is *reverse-mode automatic differentiation*. Its graph records a computation, not conditional independence. End by summarizing the chain rule and accumulation.
5. **Learn through small batches of observations — 13 minutes.** Explain why a uniformly selected batch provides an unbiased estimate of the full-data gradient at fixed weights. Distinguish computing a gradient from subtracting a step times that gradient. Describe the repeated training cycle and the effect of a learning rate that is too large or small. End with what batch learning does and what it does not guarantee.

## 2. Mathematical spine

Use `r=wᵀx` for the weighted predictor, `θ` for a scalar distribution coordinate and `μ=E[Y|x]` for the response mean. In a canonical GLM, `θ=r`. The positive dispersion `φ` scales variance and is fixed here. Avoid using θ for both this coordinate and all trainable weights. Logarithms are natural, so losses are in nats.

| Mathematical commitment | Purpose and treatment |
|---|---|
| `p(y\|x,w,φ)=h(y,φ)exp{[yθ−A(θ)]/φ}` | State/motivate the conditional exponential-family form. `A` is the log normalizing function; h is the remaining factor. Apply L02 results `μ=A′(θ)`, `Var(Y\|x)=φA″(θ)`; do not rederive them. |
| Canonical link `ℓ(μ)=(A′)⁻¹(μ)=r` | Motivate/compare: a link maps the response mean to its predictor coordinate. Gaussian `A=θ²/2`, `φ=σ²`, mean r; Bernoulli `A=log(1+e^θ)`, `φ=1`, mean sigmoid(r); Poisson `A=e^θ`, `φ=1`, mean `e^r`. Their variances are `σ²`, `μ(1−μ)` and μ. Work one Poisson prediction. |
| Alternative Bernoulli probit link `μ=Φ(r)` | State/explain: Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution; the canonical coordinate `logit(μ)` then differs from r. Numerical comparison optional. Do not apply the canonical-gradient shortcut indiscriminately. |
| `L(w)=−N⁻¹Σ_n log p(y_n\|x_n,w,φ)`; `∇L=(Nφ)⁻¹Σ_n x_n(μ_n−y_n)` | Derive one scalar likelihood chain and apply it. Log-likelihood gradient has the opposite sign, `φ⁻¹Σx(y−μ)`. φ is fixed and h independent of w. |
| Canonical loss convexity | State only; explain briefly why insufficient predictor rank or separated binary labels can prevent a unique/finite maximum-likelihood estimate. No Hessian, second-order algorithm or convergence proof. |
| `z=Wx+b`; `p_c=e^z_c/Σ_j e^z_j`; target vector `t=onehot(y)` | Motivate/work class probabilities. One-hot means one at the observed class and zero elsewhere. W has shape C×D; b,z,p,t have length C. All-score shifts leave probabilities unchanged. |
| `ℓ(z,t)=logΣ_j e^z_j−tᵀz`; `∇_zℓ=p−t` | Work the short log-normalizer derivative. For rows X:N×D and probability/target tables P,T:N×C, apply `∇_W L=N⁻¹(P−T)ᵀX`, `∇_bL=N⁻¹Σ_n(p_n−t_n)`. This vector application is distinct from the scalar formula. |
| Stable `logΣe^z=m+logΣe^(z−m)`, `m=max z` | State/explain exact identity. A loss expecting logits must receive logits, not already normalized probabilities. No numerical-analysis survey. |
| Backward contribution `bar(v_i)+=bar(v_j)∂v_j/∂v_i`, seed `bar(L)=1` | Work one complete branching binary-loss graph. `bar(v)` means the loss derivative with respect to intermediate v. Add every path contribution. Recognize numerical differencing and symbolic algebra as different methods; no full differentiation taxonomy. |
| `g_B=\|B\|⁻¹Σ_(n∈B)∇ℓ_n`; `E[g_B\|ψ]=∇L(ψ)`; `ψ_next=ψ−ηg_B` | Motivate with a brief expectation argument for fresh uniform indices sampled independently of the fixed parameter tuple ψ. This exact conditional-unbiased statement is not claimed for every shuffled-batch scheme. Apply one training cycle; no optimizer convergence theory. |
| Optional prior objective `L_MAP=L−N⁻¹log p(w)` | Recognize only, correctly scale prior relative to a mean data loss. L11 owns the substantive Gaussian-prior connection. |

L06 inherits fully observed likelihood optimization; L07 adds posterior curvature; L09 adds gradients through a parameter-dependent random draw; L11/L12 reuse the training cycle. These additional topics are not established here.

## 3. Notebook handoff — one three-species Iris fit

This is the first required **PyTorch** session, the numerical library used for tensors and automatic differentiation. Students know Python/NumPy, but must be taught the new tensor/loss/update interface. Fit all three species using the two petal measurements and an affine softmax model. Keeping all three classes is required; a four-feature refit is optional.

**Students write/manipulate:** model and loss dimensions, one manual batch-gradient calculation checked against automatic differentiation, and the forward/reset-gradients/backward/update cycle. They interpret held-out probabilities, mean negative log likelihood and classification error. These measure different things: a correct class label alone does not say how much probability the model assigned.

**Supplied:** data loading, fixed training/test split, standardization using training data only, seed/CPU settings, random batch indices, plotting and a bounded training scaffold. Use one stable logits-based loss API, floating-point predictors and integer class labels; the one-hot table is needed only for the manual check. ## 4. Concrete examples and source checks

All numerical calculations below are course-created applications of the source formulas.

- **Same predictor, different responses:** at r=0, Gaussian/Bernoulli/Poisson means are 0, 1/2 and 1, with variances `σ²`, 1/4 and 1. This makes the family's response range and uncertainty visible.
- **One count:** `x=(1,2)`, `w=(log2,0)`, `y=3`, `φ=1`. Predicted mean and variance are 2; the observation loss gradient is `(−1,−2)`. Adding `log2` to r doubles the expected count; an expected count is not a deterministic outcome.
- **Three classes:** scores `(log2,0,0)`, observed class 3 and `x=(1,2)` give probabilities `(1/2,1/4,1/4)`, loss `log4` and score gradient `(1/2,1/4,−3/4)`. The weight gradient is its outer product with x.
- **Complete backward calculation:** `x=(1,2)`, `w=(0,0)`, `y=1`, `r=0`, loss `log(1+exp(r))−yr=log2`. The log-normalizer branch contributes 1/2 to the r derivative, and the target branch contributes −1. Adding gives −1/2, hence weight derivative `(−1/2,−1)`. This is the only complete differentiation graph; it is not Murphy's Fig. 6.1.
- **Iris:** retain the L01 three-species context, now fitting probabilities. Source figures/embedded output are not guarantees for the adaptation. Roughly 5–7 conceptual visual groups suffice; no layout or asset is specified here.

**Text sources:** Book 2 §§15.1/15.1.1.1–15.1.1.3/15.1.2/15.1.3, pp. 591–595, supply scalar families, links and gradient; §15.3.2, pp. 611–612, Eqs. 15.135–15.137, supplies the categorical model. §§6.2.2.1–6.2.2.2, especially pp. 268–270/Algorithm 6.4, supply backward differentiation; §§6.3.1–6.3.2, pp. 271–273, supply finite-data SGD. §§14.1.2/14.2.1, pp. 576–578, supply the likelihood/log-score bridge. **Source cautions:** Book 2 Eq. 15.35 is a log-likelihood derivative with unit dispersion; minimizing negative log likelihood reverses its sign and fixed dispersion restores the scale above. Convexity does not by itself prove finite, uniquely identified coefficients. The book's binomial parameter is a success probability, whereas the response mean is number-of-trials times that probability; our binary response has one trial. A differentiation graph is not a probabilistic graph.
